Read the methods

Five steps, in this order

  1. Listen first. Ask what people need before offering anything. Hold separate, safe conversations with women, young people, displaced families and others who are often left out.
  2. Check safety. Confirm the land is free of explosive hazards, map what divides and connects people, and make sure nothing can reach armed actors.
  3. Find evidence. Look at what worked elsewhere, how strong that evidence is, and what it cannot tell you.
  4. Start small. Take the bridge’s safer first step, with consent, a complaint route and a plan for upkeep.
  5. Measure and share back. Measure what the community said matters, share results with them first, and change course when needed.

Evidence library

Publications from UN agencies, research institutes, standard-setters and peer-reviewed journals. Each entry says what kind of source it is, how strong its evidence is and what it cannot tell you. Every link was checked on 21 October 2026. Sources open on their publishers’ websites and need an internet connection.

How to read evidence strength
A Strong
Several independent, rigorous studies agree.
B Moderate
A rigorous study, a large-data analysis, or a review of many studies.
C Emerging
Before-and-after results, case studies or programme experience, without a comparison group.
D Untested here
An idea or proposal with no measured results yet in this context. Every bridge starts here.
How-to
Guidance, standards and law tell you how to act well. They are not proof that something works.
Context
Situation reports describe a problem. They do not test solutions.

These grades are editorial labels written for this tool. They are not an independent systematic appraisal. Publication dates are not the dates when impacts were observed.

BPeer-reviewed study

Armed Conflict Accelerates Cropland Loss and Food Insecurity Worldwide

Combines annual 30-metre global cropland maps (2000–2020) with more than 460,000 conflict events in 155 countries. Using difference-in-differences and propensity score matching, it finds that conflict-affected areas lost 23.7% more cropland than comparable areas, with losses lasting for years.

Nature Food. 24 September 2026.

Keep in mind: One global study of land within 5 km of conflict events. It measures association with careful controls; it cannot explain every local cause.

Solution bridges

Eight proposed pathways from documented experience to communities that ask for help. None of them is an existing partnership or a funded project. Each one needs an independent conflict and ecological risk assessment and the community’s free, prior and informed consent.

Farmer-managed natural regeneration for land and livelihood recovery

Help trees regrow from living stumps and roots already in farmers’ fields, so tired land recovers at low cost and families gain shade, fodder, firewood and better harvests.

Where it comes from

Farmer-led regreening in Niger, where an estimated 200 million trees regrew across about 5 million hectares of farmland, spreading from farmer to farmer over two decades.

Who it could help

Degraded farmland where living stumps and roots remain, where the community has asked for support, and where the people doing the work can keep the benefits of the trees.

What must be true first

  • Clear, fair agreement on who owns and benefits from regrown trees, including women and tenant farmers.
  • Agreement with herders on grazing, livestock corridors and dry-season access.
  • Confirmation from a mine action authority that the land is safe to work, wherever fighting took place.
  • Rainfall, soil and rootstock that can support regrowth, checked with local knowledge.

What could go wrong

  • Disputes over who owns regrown trees, or land grabs as land values rise.
  • Blocked livestock routes that raise farmer–herder tension.
  • Women losing access to trees, fodder or firewood they relied on.
  • Injuries from explosive remnants of war when land is worked.

How to prevent harm

  • Run free, prior and informed consent on the community’s own terms, and respect a ‘no’.
  • Recognise customary and informal rights before any work starts.
  • Include pastoralists in writing the rules, and keep corridors open.
  • Set up a complaint and dispute route that people trust.

Smaller, safer first step

Start with demonstration plots on volunteers’ land and exchange visits. Change nobody’s land rights, and ask for no labour beyond what volunteers offer.

Solar-powered community water for families and schools

Use solar pumps to give families and schools reliable, safe water, run by a committee that fairly represents everyone who uses it.

Where it comes from

Decentralised solar water systems used in humanitarian and rural settings, and water cases in UNDP’s climate solutions for peacebuilding.

Who it could help

Neighbourhoods, settlements and schools where water services were damaged or never existed, and where residents ask for a shared, fairly governed supply.

What must be true first

  • A survey of how much water the source can safely give, and water-quality testing.
  • A water committee with women, displaced families and host residents in real decision-making roles.
  • Spare parts, trained local technicians and a maintenance fund for at least three years.
  • Safe siting away from military positions, with safe, well-lit paths, especially for women and girls.

What could go wrong

  • Armed actors seizing control of the water point or taxing access.
  • Over-pumping that dries wells or springs other people rely on.
  • Some groups being excluded, sparking conflict between host and displaced communities.
  • Unsafe journeys to water, including the risk of gender-based violence.
  • Breakdowns with no parts or funds, leaving people worse off than before.

How to prevent harm

  • Follow Sphere minimum standards for water quantity, quality and access.
  • Run a Do No Harm analysis of who could gain power by controlling water.
  • Never publish exact locations of water points in conflict areas.
  • Offer a confidential complaint channel that bypasses the committee.

Smaller, safer first step

Add solar power to one existing, working borehole with an inclusive committee in place, and measure uptime, queues and fairness for six months before expanding.

Evidence behind this bridge

Shared land agreements and agroforestry for farmer–herder cooperation

Bring farmers and herders together to agree on land, water and routes, plant trees and fodder that benefit both, and train local mediators to settle disputes early.

Where it comes from

Cross-border peacebuilding between Sierra Leone and Guinea, where reported farmer–herder disputes fell sharply but local committees and planted grass had mixed results, plus CGIAR’s climate security assessment tool.

Who it could help

Communities where farming and herding overlap and both groups ask for help to reduce clashes.

What must be true first

  • Consent from both farmers and pastoralists, through representatives each group chooses.
  • Recognised livestock corridors and access to water points.
  • Trusted local mediators, including women.
  • Seasonal calendars of planting, harvest and herd movements.

What could go wrong

  • Committees captured by one group or by local elites. In Sierra Leone only 20.4% said committees work for everyone equally.
  • Planted fodder failing in the dry season, as it did on all five Sierra Leone ranches.
  • Monitoring stopping when outside incentives end.
  • Published route maps exposing herders to raids or theft.

How to prevent harm

  • Track the fairness of committees separately for each group, and act on the results.
  • Choose species with proven dry-season survival in the area.
  • Budget for monitoring after the project ends, owned by local institutions.
  • Keep route maps with the communities, and don’t publish them.

Smaller, safer first step

Start with mediated dialogue and a written agreement on one shared corridor or water point, before any planting or construction.

Home-grown school meals for child-centred recovery

Serve school meals made with food bought from local farmers, so children eat and learn while nearby farms gain a steady market.

Where it comes from

School meal programmes that now reach at least 466 million children worldwide, many of them linked to local food purchasing.

Who it could help

Schools that are open and safe, in communities that want meals and have farmers able to supply them.

What must be true first

  • Safe school buildings that are not used for military purposes.
  • Clean water, hygiene, and safe food storage and cooking.
  • A child safeguarding policy and trained staff.
  • Fair, transparent purchasing from local farmers and women’s groups.

What could go wrong

  • Schools becoming targets, or meals drawing children into unsafe journeys.
  • Food diverted or sold, or corruption in purchasing.
  • Out-of-school, displaced or disabled children being left out.
  • Firewood for cooking driving tree loss.
  • Meals stopping suddenly when funding ends.

How to prevent harm

  • Follow the Safe Schools Declaration: schools are never used for military purposes.
  • Offer take-home rations for children who can’t attend.
  • Use fuel-efficient stoves and plant woodlots.
  • Run child-friendly, confidential complaint channels.

Smaller, safer first step

Pilot in a few safe schools that already have water, buy a few staple foods locally, and plan with the community from day one for when outside funding ends.

Evidence behind this bridge

  • State of School Feeding Worldwide 2024
  • Safe Schools Declaration
  • Programme Guidance and Operational Notes: Strengthening Shock-Responsive Social Protection Systems
  • Global Report on Food Crises 2026
  • The Sphere Handbook: Humanitarian Charter and Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response

Healing landscapes: psychosocial support woven into restoration work

Combine community restoration work, like tree nurseries, gardens and water care, with basic psychosocial support, so people can recover together while repairing their surroundings.

Where it comes from

The IASC’s layered model of mental health and psychosocial support, which treats community and family supports as a core layer, and WHO’s psychological first aid.

Who it could help

Communities that want shared, practical activities, where trained people can offer basic support and referrals.

What must be true first

  • Facilitators trained in psychological first aid.
  • A referral list for specialised mental health care that actually works.
  • Voluntary participation, with no pressure to share experiences.
  • Separate groups where needed (women, men, youth), and child-friendly spaces run by trained staff.

What could go wrong

  • Pushing people to talk about painful events, which can cause harm.
  • Labelling people as ‘traumatised’ or creating stigma.
  • Unpaid work presented as therapy.
  • Raising expectations of care that isn’t available.

How to prevent harm

  • Make no clinical claims. This is support, not treatment.
  • Pay fairly for work, and keep support activities voluntary.
  • Use only validated, locally adapted wellbeing measures, with ethical review.
  • Protect privacy: no names or stories shared without informed consent.

Smaller, safer first step

Begin with one nursery or garden group led by trained local facilitators and backed by a tested referral route, before adding any structured wellbeing activities.

Evidence behind this bridge

  • IASC Guidelines on Mental Health and Psychosocial Support in Emergency Settings
  • Psychological First Aid: Guide for Field Workers
  • Principles for Ecosystem Restoration to Guide the United Nations Decade 2021–2030
  • Core Humanitarian Standard on Quality and Accountability (2024 edition)

Shock-responsive cash for community-chosen restoration

Pay fair wages, through trusted social protection systems, for restoration work the community chooses, so families can buy food while land and water systems recover.

Where it comes from

UNICEF’s 2026 guidance on shock-responsive social protection, and climate–peace programme experience reviewed by UNDP.

Who it could help

Households facing hunger or lost income where cash can be delivered safely and local markets still work.

What must be true first

  • Safe payment channels that armed actors can’t tax or control.
  • Fair selection rules that people understand and can challenge.
  • Wages equal to local rates, and no work for children below the legal working age.
  • Support for people who can’t do physical work, such as older people, people with disabilities and pregnant women.

What could go wrong

  • Local elites capturing the beneficiary lists.
  • Payments seen as favouring one group, fuelling tension.
  • Unsafe work on contaminated land.
  • Personal data on beneficiaries exposed or misused.
  • Price rises if local markets can’t supply goods.

How to prevent harm

  • Check that markets can supply goods before using cash.
  • Run explosive ordnance checks before any land work.
  • Minimise payment data, protect it, and delete it when no longer needed.
  • Watch for tensions and adjust selection with the community.

Smaller, safer first step

Start with one small, community-chosen project and a short payment cycle, with clear rules and an independent complaint route.

Evidence behind this bridge

Community seed systems for food sovereignty after displacement

Help farmers recover diverse, locally adapted seed and share it fairly, so families can replant after losing harvests, tools and seed stocks.

Where it comes from

Research showing that conflict destroys crops, seed stocks and markets, and that cropland loss can last for years without recovery support.

Who it could help

Farming communities returning to their land, or hosting displaced farmers, who say seed is what stops them replanting.

What must be true first

  • An assessment, with farmers, of whether the problem is seed availability, money to buy it, or seed quality.
  • Varieties farmers know and can save and replant.
  • Working local markets and traders, where they exist.
  • Safe access to fields.

What could go wrong

  • Unsuitable or non-local seed that fails or can’t be saved.
  • Free handouts undercutting local seed traders.
  • Women farmers excluded from seed fairs or banks.
  • Seed banks controlled by a few powerful people.

How to prevent harm

  • Prefer local varieties that farmers choose.
  • Buy locally where markets work.
  • Make sure women and displaced farmers have a voice and access.
  • Keep seed bank records simple, open and local.

Smaller, safer first step

Hold a seed needs discussion with farmers, then one community seed fair buying from local suppliers, before any large distribution.

Community-led restoration monitoring for shared, trusted proof

Let communities measure restoration themselves, with simple indicators they choose, checked against satellite images, so results are trusted and owned locally.

Where it comes from

WRI’s monitoring, reporting and verification approach, field-tested with more than 200 locally led restoration projects.

Who it could help

Any community running restoration that wants to show results to itself, funders and authorities without giving up control of its data.

What must be true first

  • Community agreement on what to measure and who may see the data.
  • Simple tools that work offline.
  • A data protection plan: what is collected, where it is stored, who can see it and when it is deleted.
  • Clear rules for location data in conflict areas.

What could go wrong

  • Precise maps or photos revealing homes, people or assets to armed actors.
  • Data used for land grabs or surveillance.
  • Outsiders taking the data and the credit.
  • Monitoring turning into box-ticking for funders.

How to prevent harm

  • Publish only combined, coarse-resolution data.
  • No people, faces or homes in photos without consent, and never children’s faces.
  • Written data agreements that keep the community in control.
  • Keep raw data on local devices and delete what isn’t needed.

Smaller, safer first step

Choose three community-defined indicators and fixed photo points without people, and report results only at village or district level.

Healing and peace practices

Twelve well-established ways of working that keep people safe, support healing and build trust. Use them alongside any bridge. Each one links to the guidance it comes from.

Do No Harm: dividers and connectors

Before and during any project, look at what pulls people apart and what holds them together, and make sure your work strengthens the second.

  1. List the dividers: tensions, grievances, unfair access and anything that separates groups.
  2. List the connectors: markets, water points, shared values, schools and people who bridge groups.
  3. Look at your project’s actions (who gets what, who decides, who is hired) and your team’s behaviour.
  4. Ask how each action affects dividers and connectors, then change what makes things worse.

Take care: Write about issues, not individuals. Don’t name people or armed groups, or record where they are.

Psychological first aid: look, listen, link

Simple, humane support anyone can learn to offer people in distress, without forcing them to talk.

  1. Look: check for safety, urgent basic needs and people who are very distressed.
  2. Listen: approach gently, ask what people need and what worries them, and help them feel calm.
  3. Link: help people meet basic needs, get information, and connect with loved ones and services.

Take care: Never press anyone to describe what happened. Don’t promise what you can’t deliver. Know where to refer people.

Layers of support for mental health and wellbeing

Healing happens in layers: safety and basic services for everyone, community and family support for many, and specialised care for some.

  1. Basic services and security, delivered in ways that protect dignity.
  2. Community and family supports, such as group activities and restored routines.
  3. Focused, non-specialised supports from trained workers.
  4. Specialised services, with referral routes that work.

Take care: Most people recover with good community support. Don’t label people, and don’t start clinical work without qualified staff.

Free, prior and informed consent

People decide for themselves, with full information and no pressure, whether a project goes ahead on their land, and they can say no.

  1. Identify the rights-holders and the representatives they choose.
  2. Map land and resource use together, through participatory mapping.
  3. Share information in local languages and formats, and discuss it over time.
  4. Reach consent through the community’s own process, and agree a complaints mechanism.
  5. Monitor the agreement together and record lessons with the community.

Take care: Consent is not a signature. It can be given in stages, given with conditions, or withdrawn.

Land and tree rights first

Make sure the people who restore land keep the benefits, and that no one loses land or access because of the project.

  1. Recognise all legitimate tenure, including customary, informal and communal rights and women’s use rights.
  2. Consult everyone whose rights may be affected before decisions are made.
  3. Agree in writing who benefits from regrown trees, crops and water.
  4. Provide a fair route to resolve disputes.

Take care: Restoration can raise land values and attract land grabs. Watch for this from the start.

Safe ground: explosive ordnance checks before land work

Land where fighting took place may hide mines or unexploded weapons. Find out before anyone digs, plants or drills.

  1. Ask the national mine action authority or an accredited mine action organisation whether the area has been surveyed.
  2. Arrange explosive ordnance risk education for workers and families before work starts.
  3. Never touch, move or burn suspicious objects. Mark the place and report it.
  4. Stop work immediately if anything suspicious is found.

Take care: Restoration teams never carry out clearance themselves. Only accredited organisations do.

Keep schools safe for learning

Schools are for children. Keep them free from military use and plan school activities so they don’t put children at risk.

  1. Keep schools for learning, never for military or armed security purposes.
  2. Plan safe routes and times for children.
  3. Have a safety and safeguarding plan for every school-based activity.
  4. Report attacks on schools or their military use through appropriate channels.

Take care: Don’t publish school locations or children’s photos from conflict areas.

Women in decision-making

Women have the right to shape decisions about peace and recovery, not just to be consulted at the end.

  1. Hold separate sessions with women at times and places that are safe and convenient, with childcare.
  2. Give women decision-making roles on committees, not token seats.
  3. Check who controls the benefits: water, trees, cash, land.
  4. Partner with local women’s organisations.

Take care: Assess with women themselves whether taking part could expose them to backlash, and plan for it.

Young people as peacebuilders

Young people are partners in building peace, with ideas, energy and a stake in the future.

  1. Invite young people into planning, with real roles and real responsibility.
  2. Create paid, skilled work in restoration, such as nurseries, monitoring and water maintenance.
  3. Support youth-led organisations.
  4. Make reintegration and livelihoods open to young people affected by conflict.

Take care: Don’t treat young people as a security threat, and don’t use them as unpaid labour.

Accountability to the people you serve

People affected by a project should be able to give feedback, complain safely, and see that it changes things.

  1. Ask people how they want to receive information and give feedback.
  2. Set up safe, confidential complaint channels, including for children and for sexual exploitation and abuse.
  3. Respond to every complaint and show what changed.
  4. Share information about the project, its budget and its limits in local languages.

Take care: Complaints about staff misconduct need a protected route that bypasses the staff involved.

Restore with people, not for them

Good restoration is planned and led with local people, uses every kind of knowledge, and is cared for long after the project ends.

  1. Bring in inclusive, participatory decision-making from the very start.
  2. Combine local, Indigenous and scientific knowledge.
  3. Fit the work to the local ecology, culture and economy, and to the wider landscape.
  4. Monitor and adapt during and after the project.

Take care: Planting numbers are not success. Survival, fairness and local ownership are.

Positive peace lens

Peace is more than the absence of violence. It is built by fair institutions, shared resources and trust.

  1. Use the eight pillars as a checklist: which could this bridge strengthen?
  2. Ask which pillars it could weaken, for example through corruption in purchasing.
  3. Choose one or two local signs of change to track over time.

Take care: National peace indices are not designed to judge a single project.

Bridge planner

Work through a bridge with your community or team. Answers save on this device only, nothing is sent anywhere, and you can export a backup or a one-page brief at any time. Describe issues and groups, never individuals, and use a general area name rather than an exact location.

The interactive planner needs JavaScript. You can still use its questions on paper or in a meeting:

Consent and voice

  1. Essential: Has the community, through representatives it chose itself, asked for or agreed to this idea?
  2. Essential: Can people say no, or withdraw later, without losing any aid or services?
  3. Have women, young people, displaced families and other often-excluded groups been heard in ways that are safe for them?

Safety and do no harm

  1. Essential: Has a mine action authority or accredited organisation confirmed the land is safe to work, or that it was never contaminated?
  2. Essential: Have you checked that no money, food, water or control will reach armed actors?
  3. Have you mapped what divides and what connects people here, and how this project could affect both?
  4. Is there a safe, confidential way for anyone, including children, to raise a complaint?

Land, water and rights

  1. Essential: Are land, tree and water rights clear enough that the people doing the work will keep the benefits?
  2. If Indigenous Peoples are affected, has a free, prior and informed consent process started on their terms?
  3. Do herders, farmers and other users of the same land or water agree on access and routes?

Ecological fit

  1. Has someone with local knowledge confirmed that rainfall, soil and water can support this?
  2. Will you use native or locally proven species and avoid invasive ones?
  3. Is there a water budget showing the project won’t drain wells or springs that others rely on?

Local ownership and upkeep

  1. Is there a local group, not only an outside organisation, that will run and maintain this?
  2. Are spare parts, skills and money for upkeep planned for at least three years?
  3. Could this start small and grow only if it works?

Measuring and sharing back

  1. Has the community described what success looks like, in its own words?
  2. Will you take a starting measurement (a baseline) before work begins?
  3. Will results be shared back with the community in a usable form, without exposing anyone’s identity or exact location?

Who is in the room?

  • Women
  • Girls and boys (through child-safe methods, with caregivers’ consent)
  • Young people
  • Older people
  • People with disabilities
  • Displaced families
  • Host community
  • Farmers
  • Herders and pastoralists
  • Indigenous Peoples (where present)
  • Language, religious or ethnic minorities
  • Local authorities
  • Traditional and faith leaders
  • Local organisations, including women’s groups

Methods

How evidence is judged, how ideas are tested for fit, and how to measure results without putting anyone at risk. Each method opens with a plain summary.

Evidence strength and uncertainty

Say how sure we are, and why.

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Grade A means independently replicated measured outcomes. Grade B means a rigorous single study, a large-data analysis or a review of many studies. Grade C means case studies, programme experience or before-and-after results without a comparison group. Grade D means a proposal or hypothesis. Guidance, standards and law are marked “How-to”, and situation reports are marked “Context”, because neither tests whether something works.

Never present targets or plans as achieved results. For every figure, record the publication date, the measurement period, the place, the method and the likely confounders. A publication date is not the date an impact was observed.

Ecological transferability

Will this work with this land, water and climate?

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Compare the origin and the proposed site on agro-ecological zone, rainfall, soil, native biodiversity, water budget, land tenure, grazing routes, invasive species risk, climate projections, labour and long-term maintenance. Prefer locally validated species and traditional knowledge. Where conditions differ, design a small trial rather than assuming results will travel.

Peace-positive evaluation

Does this reduce tension, or could it add to it?

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Use participatory conflict mapping, the Do No Harm dividers-and-connectors analysis, inclusive consent, analysis of who holds power, and a working dispute-resolution route. Track unintended effects. Look at how benefits and burdens fall on women, children, displaced residents, farmers, pastoralists, people with disabilities and minorities. The positive peace pillars help name lasting changes worth tracking.

Measurement and verification

Measure what the community says matters, and check it honestly.

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Combine community-defined outcomes with field surveys, baselines, comparison areas where ethical, coarse-scale remote sensing and clear uncertainty ranges. Useful measures include safe-water functionality, dietary diversity, school attendance, vegetation survival, household income, complaints and conflict incidents. Publish only combined figures that cannot identify a person, household or facility.

Funding and procurement integrity

Make sure money reaches people, not armed groups or middlemen.

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Require beneficial-ownership checks, sanctions screening, independent procurement, safeguarding policies, audit trails and controls against diversion. Never trade humanitarian access for political endorsement or favourable publicity. Publish budgets in summary so communities can see where money goes.

Data ethics and location minimisation

Collect less, protect it, delete it.

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Collect only what a decision needs. Record places at the coarsest level that still works, such as district rather than GPS point. Keep raw data on local devices under community agreement, protect it, and set a deletion date. Never publish precise locations of at-risk people, schools, shelters, clinics or water points. Photos should not show faces or homes without informed consent, and never children’s faces in conflict settings.

Research and development opportunities

Promising ideas to test, not proven solutions.

Set Reading level to Full detail under Display to read the full method.

Candidates for careful evaluation include decentralised solar water systems, farmer-managed natural regeneration in post-conflict areas, climate-security sensitivity tools, causal impact evaluation, privacy-preserving satellite verification of restoration, local-language knowledge exchange, community seed systems after displacement, combined restoration and psychosocial support, and conflict-sensitive cash and social protection. Treat emerging technologies as things to evaluate, not established solutions.

Zero Harm and Anti-Coercion covenant

These commitments apply to everyone who uses this tool and every bridge it describes. They are not optional, and no funding, deadline or partnership outweighs them.

Protect identities and locations

Never identify vulnerable individuals, publish the precise locations of at-risk children, families or facilities, or collect information that could be used to find, profile or pressure anyone.

Aid is never a bargaining chip

Support is impartial and independent. It is never conditional on taking part, political or religious loyalty, unpaid labour, information about others, or appearing in publicity. People can say no or withdraw without losing anything.

Nothing for armed actors

No money, goods, water, land or control may be routed to armed actors, and no project may be run in a way that strengthens their hold over civilians.

Safe ground before work

No digging, planting, drilling or clearing on land affected by fighting until a mine action authority or accredited organisation confirms it is safe.

Children first

Follow child safeguarding policies, keep schools free from military use, never use children’s images or stories without informed consent from the child and caregiver, and never in ways that could identify them.

Land and resource justice

Screen every proposal for displacement, dispossession, greenwashing, retaliation and resource conflict. Seek free, prior and informed consent, and respect a no.

Honesty about evidence

Never present a target as a result, a proposal as proven, or a partner’s endorsement that has not been given. Listing a publication here does not mean its authors endorse this tool.

Listen and answer

Every bridge needs a safe, confidential complaint route, including for children and for sexual exploitation and abuse, and a visible response.

What this tool will never do

  • Store or show maps of vulnerable people, facilities or routes.
  • Rank, score or profile communities or individuals.
  • Send your plans, searches or settings anywhere. There are no accounts, analytics or trackers.
  • Load code, fonts or images from other websites.

Glossary

Plain-language meanings for the terms used on this page.

Agroforestry
Growing trees together with crops or livestock on the same land, so each helps the other.
Baseline
A measurement taken before work starts, so you can see later what changed.
Bridge (in this tool)
A proposal to adapt something that worked in one place for a community elsewhere that asks for it. It stays a proposal until it has been tested there.
Conflict sensitivity
Understanding the conflict around you and making sure your work reduces tension rather than adding to it.
Connectors and dividers
Connectors bring people together, like shared markets or values. Dividers pull them apart, like unfair access or old grievances.
Do No Harm
A method for checking how help can accidentally fuel conflict, and for changing course so it builds peace instead.
Elite capture
When a few powerful people take control of a project’s benefits meant for everyone.
Evidence strength
How much weight you can put on a source’s main claims. This tool uses A (strong) to D (untested here).
Explosive ordnance
Mines, unexploded bombs, shells and other explosive remnants of war that can stay dangerous for decades.
Explosive ordnance risk education
Teaching people how to recognise, avoid and report explosive hazards.
FMNR
Farmer-managed natural regeneration: helping trees regrow from living stumps, roots and seeds already in the soil.
FPIC
Free, prior and informed consent: people’s right to decide, without pressure and with full information, whether a project affecting their land goes ahead.
Grievance mechanism
A safe way for people to raise complaints and get a response.
Home-grown school feeding
School meals made with food bought from local farmers.
International humanitarian law
The rules of war that protect civilians, people no longer fighting, and the natural environment.
Land tenure
The rules, written or customary, about who can use land, for how long and under what conditions.
Location minimisation
Recording places only as precisely as you truly need, so data can’t be used to find or target people.
MHPSS
Mental health and psychosocial support: any support that protects or improves people’s wellbeing and mental health.
MRV
Monitoring, reporting and verification: measuring results, sharing them and checking they are true.
Pastoralism
Raising livestock on open land, often moving herds with the seasons to find water and grazing.
Positive peace
The attitudes, institutions and structures that create and sustain peaceful societies.
Psychological first aid
Humane, practical support for people in distress: look, listen and link them to help.
Remote sensing
Measuring changes on the ground, such as tree cover, from satellite or aerial images.
Safeguarding
Everything an organisation does to keep children and adults safe from harm, abuse and exploitation by its staff and activities.
Shock-responsive social protection
Support systems, such as cash transfers, designed to scale up quickly when a crisis hits.
Transhumance
The seasonal movement of herders and livestock between grazing areas.
Value chain
All the steps a product goes through, from farm to buyer.
Water budget
An estimate of how much water an area receives and how much can be used without running out.
